TypixDeck — the CNC-aluminum pocket Linux computer

A palm-sized, fanless Linux machine milled from a single block of 6061 aluminum. Raspberry Pi CM0 / CM4 / CM5 inside. Every screw, schematic and STEP file published.

Most small computers ask you to choose: silence or power, portability or repairability, a finished product or an open one. TypixDeck refuses the trade. The chassis is the heatsink — 0 dB under sustained load — and the whole machine opens with four captive screws.

Why we built it

A computer should be something you own — not something you are merely allowed to use.

We got tired of glued batteries, locked bootloaders and cases designed to crack open only in a repair shop. So we machined our own answer, sized it to a shirt pocket, and published every file it took to make it.

  • No fans. The aluminum body carries the heat, silently.
  • No glue. Four captive screws. Labeled connectors. A public teardown guide.
  • No black boxes. Schematics, firmware and full STEP files on GitHub, day one.
  • No lock-in. Standard Raspberry Pi compute-module socket — swap it, upgrade it, keep it.

Milled, not molded

The body starts as a solid billet of 6061-T6 aluminum and comes off a CNC mill bead-blasted and anodized. It is the structure, the shielding and the heatsink in one part.

Because the compute module bonds thermally to the chassis, the whole 280-gram body spreads the heat — a CM4 runs full-tilt at 0 dB, forever.

6061-T6 billet · bead-blasted · anodized · 95 × 62 × 22 mm

The socket is the upgrade path

TypixDeck takes any Raspberry Pi CM4 or CM5 — and even the tiny CM0, through our own castellated 132-pin adapter. When a faster module ships in three years, your computer gets faster. The chassis stays.

Inside: an M.2 slot for NVMe, microSD, and a 40-pin GPIO header for HATs and hardware experiments.

CM0 / CM4 / CM5 socketed · M.2 NVMe · 40-pin GPIO

A real keyboard, the size of a card

The companion BLE keyboard runs open ZMK firmware on an nRF52840 — full QWERTY layout, white backlight, a 6-axis air-mouse, Bluetooth and USB-C. Pair it with the deck and the whole workstation disappears into a jacket pocket.

Prefer to solder your own? The $9 DIY snap-dome kit is the same keyboard, in pieces.

Risks and challenges

TypixDeck is past the prototype stage — the chassis, mainboard and keyboard you see on this page are photographed from working hardware, and small batches have already shipped. The honest risks that remain are supply-chain ones: compute-module availability and anodizing lead times can move a batch by a few weeks.

We machine in small runs precisely so a delay affects dozens of units, not thousands. You can cancel for a full refund any time before your unit ships, and every design file is public — so even in the worst case, the hardware outlives us.
